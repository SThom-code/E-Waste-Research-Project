E-Waste: Disposal

1. Impact on environment and living conditions

Recycling (Click to view)

When collected properly, in Canada, E-waste is transported into regional processing facilities to be recycled, which allows us to manufacture new products from it while reducing the environmental impact production would’ve had if we had had to dig up new resources. The general process varies from province to province and even city to city, but most e-waste is treated as follows:

Before it can be recycled, E-waste is first sorted in regional processing facilities. This is because different devices are recycled differently. More than just sorting happens at this stage. Devices with personal data (smartphones, computers) have to be wiped to protect their previous users, and some devices will have to be dismantled so that dangerous components (batteries, lamps containing mercury–which can damage the brain and coordination) can be removed and properly handled.

All the parts then move on to recycling, where they are shredded and separated (based on conductivity, magnetism and density). Some materials are sent to be further refined under heat, such as metals or plastics, which pose a challenge since many different plastics are used in electronics. Then, they are mostly recycled into new products.

The whole process allows us to save energy, helps us reduce greenhouse gas emissions, reduces the need to mine resources and in all minimizes the impact of mining.

Improper E-Waste Management (Click to view)

While recycling is one of the best options for managing the trash, it’s not used as much as it should be, likely because of the cost. In fact, only 17-20% of E-waste is properly recycled. In 2019, 53.6 million metric tons of waste was generated, and a report suggests that 4500 Eiffel Towers could be constructed from the amount of waste generated each year.

Instead of dealing with their country's laws of E-waste management and its costs, certain companies choose to export to less developed countries and use methods such as landfilling, incineration or acid baths. This has had many harmful effects on the environment and the people living near the dumping sites.

Humans

When exposed to them for long periods of time, some components found in appliances can have disastrous effects on human health. One example is dioxins, a group of highly toxic chemicals that have been detected in the range of 15 to 56 times the World Health Organization’s recommended maximum intake near waste sites. They make their way into the body, harming respiratory health and the immune system in addition to being able to cause cancers, tumors and chronic diseases.

Environment

Incorrect treatment of e-waste can cause a lot of pollution. Chemicals in landfills can seep into the soil and contaminate the water, which is transmitted onto the plants that absorb it, giving them a variety of illnesses. This affects both humans and animals alike. The toxin-filled water can eventually make its way into rivers and ponds, spreading to areas multiple kilometers away from the facility.

2. Local, national and international policies

Local

The City of Ottawa doesn’t directly collect any form of electronic waste as a part of its curbside garbage collection. Instead, it’s recommended to seek out retailers who collect it (a list of places is available on this website).

International

The WEEE Directive: Active as of 2012 in the EU, was created to protect the environment and human health from E-waste. It does so by encouraging countries to prevent the creation of E-waste by reusing and recovering usable components from the waste.

The Basel Convention: Created on May 5th of 1992, with Canada joining in late August, the Basel Convention was a response to companies choosing to dump their trash in less regulated countries instead of in their own backyards, which had disastrous effects on the environment and people surrounding it. Therefore, the Convention’s aims are to minimize the generation of hazardous waste (such as e-waste) while making sure that the waste that has to be processed is done so properly. They do so by forbidding the transport of certain waste to countries that do not conform to their regulations.

3. What the future holds

In 2030, more than 74Mt of e-waste is anticipated to be produced. To deal with the growing problem, many solutions to the mismanagement of the disposal of e-waste have been suggested.

For example, a company called GreeNovel has created a prototype that can heat and break down non-recyclable plastics. Another, AxiPolymer, has developed an alternative to the plastic wrap used to package devices that is recyclable, which could potentially reduce greenhouse gas emissions up to 35%.

Some other proposals are:

  • Hydrometallurgy: Shredding e-waste into smaller pieces and then treating it with chemicals.
  • Biotech: Using special bacteria and fungi’s acids and enzymes to break down specific materials and separate them from others.
  • Urban mining: Extracting metals from previously discarded electronics.
  • Upcycling: Transforming old electronic components into new products, such as jewelry or new devices.
